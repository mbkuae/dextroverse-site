How it works

Seven steps. Each one hands the next a thing it can check, and each one is allowed to refuse.

  1. Describe it

    Type it or say it, in the words you already use for the part. No CAD, no drawing required.

  2. Confirm what we heard

    Every number is read back to you before anything is designed. A misheard dimension is caught by you, in seconds — not by a part in your hand.

  3. Design as code

    The part is generated as a parametric model, not a static mesh. Change a dimension and the part is rebuilt, not re-drawn.

  4. Measure it against what you asked for

    Each requirement is checked on the finished solid on its own terms: overall size, every bolt hole probed individually, bores, and the regions that must contain material. Anything we cannot measure is reported as not checked — never as passed.

  5. Price it from the real file

    The quote comes from a slice of the exact file that will be printed, not an estimate of something like it. If any input behind the number is unconfirmed, no price is offered at all.

  6. A named human releases it

    Before anything is made, a person approves the part, and the approval is a signed statement verified against their key. No signature, nothing is made.

  7. Make it, and account for it

    The part is printed from the approved file and delivered with a certificate binding the request, the spec you confirmed, every measurement taken, the files by content hash, and who approved the release.

What we do differently

A valid part is not the right part

A model can be flawless, the right size and the right weight, and still be the wrong part — a flange with no holes in it passes every general check there is. So we check the features you asked for, one at a time, instead of checking that the geometry is well-formed and calling it correct.

Nothing is claimed that was not measured

Our records say what was measured and, just as loudly, what was not. An unverifiable feature is flagged for a human rather than quietly counted as fine, and a part we cannot account for is refused rather than shipped with a gap in its paperwork.

Designed to refuse

Every gate fails closed. Missing price, missing signature, a file that does not match the one that was approved — each one stops the part. The failure mode of this pipeline is a question sent to a human, not a wrong part in a box.

Corrected by what we print

Every print is weighed and timed against what we predicted, and the gap is written down rather than rounded away. Real parts are what tell us an estimate was wrong, and they have — more than once. The platform gets more honest because things come out of it, not because a model got bigger.
